## Introduction

Urban housing pressure and rising construction costs are shrinking homes across dense cities, and governments largely regulate these compact dwellings by internal floor area alone. Hong Kong's Basic Housing Units Ordinance, for instance, sets a statutory minimum internal floor area of 8 m² for subdivided units, mostly commencing on 1 March 2026 (Hong Kong SAR Government, 2025), whereas normative scholarship on human flourishing proposes 30, 45, and 60 m² for one-, two-, and three-person households (Ulrich, 2025). How much floor area a household requires also carries major environmental stakes, as effective space use in existing housing can cut operational and embodied carbon more than envelope retrofits across many urban building types (Weber et al., 2024). Within this squeeze, transformable furniture—such as wall beds, fold-down desks, and drop-leaf tables—is widely promoted to make small homes work without expanding physical footprints (Goessler and Kaluarachchi, 2023; Daglio et al., 2023).

Despite this promotion, current regulations, design studies, and layout generators cannot establish how much usable floor area transformable furniture adds across diverse homes and households. Area-only rules count square meters of net internal area (NIA) without testing whether beds, tables, storage, and walking paths fit inside a specific plan, making size an incomplete measure of usability (Özer and Jacoby, 2022). Functional space standards and layout audits improve on area thresholds by checking rooms against furniture lists and dimensional indicators (Pedro, 2009; Yunitsyna, 2014; Mustafa and Ali, 2022; Freimane, 2022; Rakhmatillaeva, 2025; Ulrich, 2025), yet they rely on static furniture in fixed, hand-drawn arrangements and omit time-of-day sharing. Conversely, architectural and ergonomic studies of transformable interiors report large localized gains—such as roughly 60% more activity area in one 12 m² bedroom when a wall bed folds up (Bu and Che Mat, 2026), depth compression under ergonomic action domains (Zeng et al., 2026), or user-reported flexibility (Ashkevari and Farhady, 2022; Daglio et al., 2023)—but examine single rooms or designer-chosen layouts that mix furniture capability with manual drawing skill. Meanwhile, computational layout methods synthesize static arrangements (Merrell et al., 2011; Yu et al., 2011; Sun et al., 2025; Xiang et al., 2025) or sequence multi-state modules (Garg et al., 2016; Xiong et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Xiong et al., 2026), yet they do not tie shared-position furniture states to a 24-hour household schedule, guarantee walkable reachability from the entrance, or translate capacity gains into floor area across housing stocks. No existing method jointly represents who uses a home and when, which furniture can change state, and whether every scheduled arrangement is collision-free and reachable, expressed in m².

In this paper, we quantify schedule-weighted residential capacity across compact homes and translate the effect of transformable furniture into equivalent floor area (Figure 1). Our key idea is to compare each home with itself under three furniture strategies—static (S0), form only (S1, admitting transformable furniture in a single daily state), and form + time (S2, allowing transformable furniture at fixed positions to switch states between daily time slots)—giving each strategy its best optimized arrangement and checking every layout with an independent geometric validator. Specifically, we measure a functional capacity index (FCI) as the share of scheduled, weighted activity-hours served over 24 h; optimize discrete poses via constraint programming with greedy warm starts and lazy reachability cuts; independently verify zero overlap, unobstructed clearances, and 0.60 m walkable access; and map paired S2 versus S0 differences onto the static area–capacity curve to obtain equivalent-area gain ($\Delta\text{EA}$, in m²) and adequate area ($A_{\min}$ at $\text{FCI} \ge 0.95$). Optimizing each strategy within the same shell isolates furniture capability from designer skill, while separating the form effect ($\text{S1} - \text{S0}$) from the time or switching effect ($\text{S2} - \text{S1}$) distinguishes compact geometry from daily temporal reuse.

Across 2,619 compact plans and four household types, we find that the space-saving value of transformable furniture is substantial where static layouts bottleneck, but conditional on household composition and dwelling size. Most observed plans already function with static furniture, concentrating gains in two-person homes of intermediate compact size, where median identified positive $\Delta\text{EA}$ reaches 6.21 m² for couples and 5.00 m² for parent-and-child households. Because plan geometry creates wide functional spread at a given area, a 45 m² two-person cutoff disagrees with modeled S2 adequacy on 55.7% of couple and 52.7% of parent-and-child cases, mostly rejecting workable compact homes. In Hong Kong model shells, switching furniture states over the day is worth 2.24 m² of minimum adequate area for couples.

This study makes three contributions: (1) a schedule-aware, independently verifiable measure of functional capacity (FCI) translatable to equivalent floor area ($\Delta\text{EA}$) and adequate area ($A_{\min}$); (2) corpus-scale paired evidence showing where transformable form and daily switching save floor area and why the smallest and largest compact homes gain little; and (3) an empirical test of area-only housing standards, with a Hong Kong scenario demonstrating that furniture dimensions should be chosen by size band.

## Results

### A verified, schedule-aware comparison of furniture strategies

To evaluate furniture strategies across diverse residential geometries, we assembled 2,742 compact dwelling units within 8–50 m² of net internal area (NIA)—defined as interior furnishable, storage-only, walkable-only, and fixed-service floor space, excluding walls and balconies—and screened each unfurnished plan on a 0.10 m raster for unobstructed 0.60 m entrance-to-service access (Figure 1; Table 1; Figure S1). Excluding 123 units that failed this empty-geometry walkability screen yielded 2,619 evaluable dwellings: 2,109 observed plans drawn from Swiss Dwellings (1,236 plans; NIA 16.75–50.00 m², median 40.47 m²) and ResPlan (873 plans; NIA 25.56–49.96 m², median 45.48 m²), alongside 510 controlled generated shells spanning 8.00–40.00 m² NIA (median 16.00 m²) that extend coverage down to Hong Kong's 8 m² statutory threshold, below the smallest observed plan at 16.75 m² NIA (Table 1; Figure S1). Each dwelling was evaluated against four 24-hour household schedules partitioned into six daily slots—single commuter, single remote worker, couple (including one remote worker and an alternate guest-dining day occurring at frequency 0.143 alongside normal days at 0.857), and parent and child (requiring two separate single sleeping surfaces)—using a dimension-audited static and transformable furniture library contextualized against 43,002 usable dimension triples from 147,702 Amazon Berkeley Objects listings (Table 1; Table 2; Table S1). Across all 2,619 dwellings, four households, and three strategies (S0, S1, and S2), the search and independent validator produced 31,428 geometrically verified layouts, retaining 94 valid zero-score layouts in which no activity furniture could be placed with reachable operating clearance.

Tracking individual homes across Night, Workday, and Dinner slots illustrates when daily state switching unlocks capacity and when plan geometry prevents it (Figure 2; Figure S2). In an 18.00 m² generated shell occupied by a couple, static furniture (S0) achieves an FCI of 0.937 with zero transformation burden ($\text{TB} = 0.00$), and form-only transformable furniture (S1) reaches 0.942 ($\text{TB} = 0.00$), because neither single-state arrangement can simultaneously fit a double sleeping surface, a dedicated daytime workspace, two-person lounging, and dining clearances without collision (Figure 2). Allowing daily state switching at fixed furniture anchors (S2; $\text{TB} = 3.00$) deploys a double wall bed at night and stows it during daytime slots, raising coverage to full capacity ($\text{FCI} = 1.000$; Figure 2). By contrast, in a 10.00 m² generated shell, no double sleeping surface fits with valid clearance and access in any slot, capping FCI at 0.577 under S0 and 0.687 under both S1 and S2 with no daily state changes ($\text{TB} = 0.00$; Figure 2; Figure S2). Similarly, in a partitioned 29.97 m² observed plan from Swiss Dwellings, narrow room dimensions and doorway keep-outs restrict FCI to 0.668 under S0 and 0.687 under S1 and S2 ($\text{TB} = 0.00$; Figure 2; Figure S2), demonstrating that interior geometry—not total floor area alone—governs whether multi-state furniture can expand usable capacity.

To confirm that paired differences reflect furniture capability rather than incomplete layout search, we benchmarked the warm-started production pipeline against standalone CP-SAT, greedy footprint placement, and standalone simulated annealing across 200 stratified unit–household pairs under S2 (Figure S3; Table S2). Every method returned 100.0% independently valid layouts across all 200 pairs. The production pipeline achieved the highest mean FCI at 0.9428 (median 1.0000; summed S0 + S1 + S2 mean solve time 53.86 s, median 10.68 s), exceeding greedy placement (mean FCI 0.8612; mean 3.47 s), standalone CP-SAT (0.8675; 38.45 s), and standalone simulated annealing (0.9341; 104.12 s, median 108.33 s; paired production-minus-annealing difference +0.0087 FCI, 95% CI [0.0016, 0.0160]). Production matched the highest FCI observed across all four search methods on 189 of the 200 pairs, with a mean shortfall of only 0.0034 FCI across the remaining 11 cases (Figure S3; Table S2).

### Most compact plans already function with static furniture

Across the 2,109 observed compact plans, static furniture (S0) already serves the entire daily activity schedule ($\text{FCI} = 1$) in the clear majority of dwellings for every household type (Figure 3; Table 3). Specifically, S0 achieves full functional capacity in 90.8% of observed plans for a single commuter, 89.1% for a single remote worker, 72.7% for a couple, and 61.0% for a parent and child—spanning 88.5%, 85.9%, 59.1%, and 43.2% in Swiss Dwellings and 94.0%, 93.6%, 92.1%, and 86.3% in the larger-footprint ResPlan sample (Table 3). Because static layouts already saturate scheduled demand in more than half of observed plans for every household—and pairs with identical S0 and S2 scores map to an exact zero area difference—the all-pair median equivalent-area gain ($\Delta\text{EA}$) on observed plans is 0 m² (support bounds 0.00–0.00 m²) across all four household schedules (Table 3). Even across the 510 generated shells, which deliberately concentrate sampling in 8–16 m² footprints, S0 provides full functional capacity ($\text{FCI} = 1$) for 54.9% of single commuters and 53.9% of single remote workers (yielding 0.00–0.00 m² all-pair median $\Delta\text{EA}$), compared with 31.6% for couples (all-pair median $\Delta\text{EA}$ 1.34 m²) and 26.7% for parent-and-child households (all-pair median bounds 0.83–0.86 m²; Table 3). Transformable furniture therefore acts not as an across-the-board space multiplier, but as a targeted intervention for plans and households where static furniture hits a geometric ceiling.

### Gains concentrate in two-person households at intermediate sizes

Allowing transformable form and daily state switching (S2 versus S0) improves FCI in a small fraction of single-person observed plans—7.5% (159 of 2,109) for the single commuter and 8.7% (184 of 2,109) for the single remote worker—but in a substantial share of two-person observed plans: 23.5% (496 of 2,109) for the couple and 35.9% (757 of 2,109) for the parent and child (Figure 3; Figure 4; Table 3). Among improved observed-plan pairs whose S0 and S2 scores invert within the interior support of the observed-plan static reference curve, the median identified positive $\Delta\text{EA}$ is 1.33 m² (95% CI [0.75, 3.11], $n = 160$ identified plus 24 support-bounded pairs; improved-group median lower bound $\ge 1.33\text{ m}^2$) for the single remote worker, 6.21 m² ([4.20, 10.00], $n = 448$ identified plus 48 bounded; lower bound $\ge 6.21\text{ m}^2$) for the couple, and 5.00 m² ([4.20, 8.00], $n = 721$ identified plus 36 bounded; lower bound $\ge 5.00\text{ m}^2$) for the parent and child (Table 3). For the single commuter, all 159 improved observed-plan pairs invert at the lower boundary of the observed-plan static curve (16.75 m²), leaving the point-identified median unavailable and establishing a support-limited lower bound of $\ge 2.00\text{ m}^2$ among improved pairs (Table 3). Likewise, for the six observed-plan couple pairs in the smallest observed bin (16–18 m²), the observed-plan reference yields a median $\Delta\text{EA}$ lower bound of 16.6 m² with no finite upper bound, whereas re-inverting those same scores on the pooled static reference (which incorporates generated shells down to 8 m²) identifies a median gain of 8.2 m² (Figure 4).

Evaluating the controlled generated shells (8.00–40.00 m² NIA) resolves the complete size profile and reveals a nonmonotonic relationship between dwelling area and space savings (Figure 4). Rather than peaking in the smallest shells, two-person equivalent-area gains start small, peak at intermediate compact sizes, and decline to zero once static layouts fit. For a couple in generated shells, the all-pair median $\Delta\text{EA}$ rises from 0.75 m² (9.2% of NIA) at 8–10 m² to a peak of 7.32 m² (49.8% of NIA) at 14–16 m², before declining to 0 m² in evaluated bins from 24 m² onward (Figure 4). Similarly, for a parent and child, the all-pair median $\Delta\text{EA}$ increases from 0.69 m² (7.6% of NIA) at 8–10 m² to 6.00 m² (33.3% of NIA) at 18–20 m² and reaches 0 m² from 28 m² onward (Figure 4). In the tightest 8–10 m² shells, even stowed or deployed transformable footprints and operating clearances cannot fit alongside wet-service access, limiting how much capacity transformability can add. Measured at the adequacy crossing ($\text{FCI} \ge 0.95$), S2 reduces the minimum adequate area $A_{\min}$ on observed plans by 10.78 m² (95% CI [9.15, 12.70], from 33.96 m² [33.21, 34.67] under S0 to 23.18 m² [21.27, 24.73] under S2) for the parent-and-child household—the one observed-plan contrast with both crossings inside observed NIA support—and reduces $A_{\min}$ across generated shells by 1.68 m² ([1.12, 2.38]) for a single commuter, 3.63 m² ([2.78, 4.77]) for a single remote worker, 5.53 m² ([4.90, 6.12]) for a couple, and 6.45 m² ([5.19, 7.75]) for a parent and child (Table 3).

### Changing form and changing over time help different households

Decomposing the total functional gain ($\text{S2} - \text{S0}$) into the form effect ($\text{S1} - \text{S0}$) and the time or switching effect ($\text{S2} - \text{S1}$) shows that households benefit through distinct physical mechanisms (Figure 3; Table 3). On observed plans, single-person improvements stem almost entirely from compact transformable form rather than slot-to-slot state changes: the mean form effect is 0.0085 FCI (95% CI [0.0071, 0.0100]) versus a mean time effect of 0.0002 FCI ([0.0000, 0.0003]) for the single commuter, and 0.0107 FCI ([0.0090, 0.0125]) versus 0.0001 FCI ([0.0000, 0.0002]) for the single remote worker (Table 3). Between the two-person households, however, the roles of form and time diverge. For the parent-and-child schedule, which requires two single sleeping surfaces at night and concurrent work and lounging in the early evening, the mean form effect on observed plans is 0.0271 FCI ([0.0249, 0.0292]) while the mean time effect is only 0.0017 FCI ([0.0013, 0.0022]), indicating that compact item footprints do nearly all the work without daily state switching. For the couple, by contrast, the mean form effect (0.0130 FCI, [0.0112, 0.0148]) and mean time effect (0.0112 FCI, [0.0094, 0.0130]) contribute almost equally on observed plans—and both remain substantial in generated shells (0.0709 [0.0621, 0.0795] and 0.0372 [0.0303, 0.0447] FCI, respectively; Table 3)—because stowing a double wall bed by day directly frees floor space for daytime remote work, dining, and two-person lounging.

Sensitivity analyses confirm that this mechanistic split is not an artifact of bed-clearance conventions or solver settings, while highlighting the acute sensitivity of compact plans to circulation width (Figure S5). Because the standard catalogue requires left and right side clearances on double beds and deployed double wall beds (Table S1), we tested a separate variant allowing mirrored one-side access across 348 small units ($\le 20\text{ m}^2$ NIA); the couple's mean time effect changed by only $-0.0122$ FCI (95% CI [$-0.0203$, $-0.0040$]), and across the 18 observed plans in that size band the interval included zero. Across 200 benchmark pairs, scaling operating clearances by 0.8× or 1.2×, varying solver caps between 30, 60, and 120 s, or removing single transformable items one at a time preserved household rankings, with dropping the drop-leaf table producing the largest single-item reduction in mean S2 FCI ($-0.0267$ for couples and $-0.0199$ for parent and child; Figure S5). In contrast, widening required walking circulation from 0.60 m to 0.90 m reduced mean S2 FCI by 0.5151 for the parent-and-child schedule across 49 matched units (Figure S5), showing that accessibility-grade circulation demands substantially larger shells than standard 0.60 m passage rules.

### Floor area alone misjudges functional adequacy

Testing statutory and normative floor-area cutoffs against modeled functional adequacy ($\text{FCI} \ge 0.95$) demonstrates that area thresholds frequently misclassify two-person compact homes (Figure 5; Table 4). Evaluated across all 2,109 observed plans under S2, Ulrich's (2025) 45 m² two-person benchmark disagrees with modeled adequacy on 55.7% of couple cases and 52.7% of parent-and-child cases (Table 4). Nearly all of these disagreements are false negatives—dwellings smaller than 45 m² that nonetheless serve at least 95% of scheduled activity-hours with independently verified clearances and access (1,150 false negatives versus 25 false positives for couples; 1,090 false negatives versus 22 false positives for parent and child; Table 4). Disagreement is already large under static furniture (S0), reaching 47.0% for couples (940 false negatives, 52 false positives) and 40.5% for parent-and-child households (789 false negatives, 66 false positives), and increases under S2 because transformable layouts make additional sub-45 m² plans adequate. For one-person schedules on observed plans, Ulrich's 30 m² benchmark disagrees with S2 adequacy on 10.4% of single-commuter and 11.1% of single-remote-worker cases (versus 14.5% and 16.4% under S0; Table 4).

This classification error arises because dwellings of identical floor area vary widely in usable capacity owing to room proportions, partition walls, and door and window positions—confirming Özer and Jacoby's (2022) static usability findings and extending them to scheduled and transformable layouts (Figure 5). Within narrow 2 m² NIA bins on observed plans, the interquartile range of static (S0) FCI reaches 0.205 for couples at 26–28 m² and 0.190 for parent-and-child households at 24–26 m² (Figure 5). At the opposite regulatory extreme, Hong Kong's statutory 8 m² basic housing unit minimum sits below every observed plan in Swiss Dwellings and ResPlan (all $\ge 16.75\text{ m}^2$), yielding zero negative predictions on observed plans (Table 4). On the 510 generated shells (8.00–40.00 m²), however, an 8 m² pass threshold disagrees with S2 adequacy on 30.0% of single-commuter, 27.3% of single-remote-worker, 40.4% of couple (206 false positives), and 49.6% of parent-and-child (253 false positives) cases, passing ultra-small shells that cannot support basic daily schedules (Table 4).

### What this means for Hong Kong's smallest homes

To examine how floor-area minimums, daily switching, and furniture dimensions interact in ultra-compact housing, we evaluated 390 Hong Kong model shells—30 matched aspect-ratio and opening templates at each integer NIA from 8 to 20 m²—using a shared dimension-sensitive quality index ($\text{FCI}_q$, with adequacy at $\text{FCI}_q \ge 0.95$; Figure 6). Hong Kong's 2021 Population Census records 215,709 residents and 107,371 households living in 108,202 subdivided units across 33,658 quarters (Table 1), but public tabulations omit the joint distribution of household size and floor area required to weight territory-wide stock totals; our scenario estimates therefore describe the modeled shell templates directly. With standard catalogue dimensions, moving from static (S0) to time-switched transformable furniture (S2) reduces the minimum adequate area $A_{\min}$ from 13.40 m² (95% CI [13.03, 13.73]) to 12.37 m² ([12.00, 12.70]; 12.30 m² [11.90, 12.64] under matched extra catalogue search) for a single commuter, and from 19.58 m² ([18.78, 19.96]) to 17.07 m² ([16.58, 17.64]) for a parent and child—a reduction of 2.51 m² ([1.86, 3.03]; Figure 6). For a couple, static furniture leaves $A_{\min}$ above the 20 m² grid support (only 11 of 30 shells are adequate at 20 m²), whereas moving from fixed-state (S1) to time-switched (S2) transformable furniture lowers $A_{\min}$ from 18.57 m² ([18.18, 19.02]) to 16.33 m² ([15.87, 16.83]), isolating a daily state-switching reduction of 2.24 m² ([1.66, 2.82]; Figure 6).

Testing parametric furniture kits on 8–15 m² training shells ($n = 30$) and transferring the selected fixed kits to held-out shells ($n = 15$), small observed plans ($\le 20\text{ m}^2$, $n = 15$), and the 390 Hong Kong model shells shows that custom kit sizing offers no improvement for a single commuter and creates a size-band trade-off for a couple (Figure 6). For the single commuter, the highest-ranking training kit (0.80 m single wall bed, $0.80 \times 0.45\text{ m}$ fold-down desk, $0.70 \times 0.60\text{ m}$ drop-leaf table, and 0.60 m wardrobe frontage) differed from the standard catalogue by $+0.0013$ $\text{FCI}_q$ (95% CI [$-0.0153$, $+0.0200$]) across best-saved training layouts while reaching adequacy on fewer training shells (11 of 30 versus 13 of 30), trailed the catalogue under matched extra search for the paired compact candidate ($-0.0284$ $\text{FCI}_q$, [$-0.0557$, $-0.0042$]; 7 versus 13 adequate), and was adequate on 184 of 390 Hong Kong shells versus 246 of 390 for the optimized catalogue because downsized surfaces incur quality penalties without unlocking enough small shells (Figure 6). For the couple, the top-ranked kit combined a 1.50 m double wall bed with a wider $1.20 \times 0.55\text{ m}$ fold-down desk, a $1.00 \times 0.75\text{ m}$ drop-leaf table, and 1.40 m of wardrobe frontage. On the 30 training shells, its paired mean $\text{FCI}_q$ contrast under matched search was $+0.0021$ ([$-0.0336$, $+0.0279$], confidence interval including zero; higher in 21 shells, tied in 3, lower in 6), reaching adequacy on 5 of 30 training shells versus 2 of 30 for the catalogue (capturing 7.4% of the per-shell bespoke upper bound), 3 of 15 versus 0 of 15 on held-out shells (mean contrast $-0.0242$ [$-0.0946$, $+0.0315$]), and 10 of 15 versus 8 of 15 on small observed plans ($+0.0066$ [$-0.0040$, $+0.0181$]; Figure 6). Across the 390 Hong Kong model shells, this larger couple kit lowered modeled $A_{\min}$ to 15.39 m² ([14.67, 16.19]) versus 16.12 m² ([15.70, 16.57]) for the optimized catalogue (a 0.73 m² point difference; 154 versus 132 adequate shells overall), driven by the 13–16 m² band where adequacy rose from 30 of 120 to 51 of 120 shells (+21 shells: 4 versus 0 at 13 m², 7 versus 2 at 14 m², 16 versus 10 at 15 m², and 24 versus 18 at 16 m², compared with 103 versus 102 of 120 at 17–20 m²; Figure 6). In the 8–12 m² band, however, neither fit-out achieved adequacy on any of the 150 shells (0 of 150), and the larger kit's wider pieces reduced mean $\text{FCI}_q$ from 0.500 to 0.448 (Figure 6), demonstrating that furniture dimensions should be tailored by floor-area band rather than fixed across all compact homes.

## Discussion

Across 2,619 compact residential plans and four household schedules, our analysis shows that the space-saving value of transformable furniture is substantial where static arrangements hit geometric bottlenecks, yet strictly conditional on household composition and dwelling size. Most observed compact homes already serve scheduled daily activities with static furniture, concentrating equivalent-area gains in two-person households occupying intermediate compact sizes—driven primarily by compact multi-state form for a parent and child, and by nearly equal contributions of form and daily state switching for a couple—whereas the smallest shells lack room even for stowed or deployed transformable clearances. At the same time, dwellings of identical net internal area exhibit wide differences in verified functional capacity, causing fixed area thresholds to misclassify roughly half of observed two-person cases. Floor area alone is therefore a weak proxy for whether a compact dwelling works, whereas schedule-aware geometric verification pinpoints where furniture substitutes for space and where physical footprint remains binding.

These results clarify how large localized gains reported in single-room design studies relate to whole-dwelling performance across housing stocks. Analytical and prototype studies of individual rooms—such as Bu and Che Mat's (2026) comparison of a $4 \times 3\text{ m}$ bedroom reporting roughly 60% more clearance-inclusive free activity area (from about 5–5.5 m² to about 8 m²) when a wall bed is folded, open-floor comparisons in a modeled 35 m² flat (Elmurodov and Mametova, 2026), or case-study projections of adaptive space efficiency (Goessler and Kaluarachchi, 2023)—measure open floor space under a chosen arrangement. Evaluating an approximate $4 \times 3\text{ m}$ room envelope and 12 m² generated analogues under scheduled single-person demand and whole-home body-free accounting (Figure S6) yields 7.60 m² of daytime body-free area under both S0 and S2 in the approximate room because static placement already satisfies the full schedule without forced folding, $-0.52$ to $+1.38\text{ m}^2$ across the 12 m² analogues, and a median daytime body-free area change of 0 m² across all 2,109 observed plans and 510 generated shells for both single-person schedules. Rather than contradicting single-room designs, this difference reflects measure and scope: maximizing open daytime floor space in one room answers a different question than whether a whole dwelling's sleeping, work, dining, lounging, storage, cooking, and sanitary needs are jointly reachable over 24 h. Coupling multi-state furniture and daily schedules with independent access checks thus extends static functional standards (Pedro, 2009; Yunitsyna, 2014; Ulrich, 2025), empirical plan-usability evaluations (Özer and Jacoby, 2022; Freimane, 2022; Mustafa and Ali, 2022; Rakhmatillaeva, 2025), and ergonomic depth-compression models (Zeng et al., 2026), while complementing Weber et al.'s (2024) static excess-area carbon model.

Translating functional capacity into square meters offers actionable guidance for housing regulators, interior designers, and furniture manufacturers. For regulators, fixed floor-area minimums face a sharp classification dilemma: generous cutoffs such as 45 m² reject more than a thousand observed two-person compact plans that accommodate scheduled activities with verified clearances, whereas ultra-low minimums such as 8 m² pass shells in which basic one- and two-person schedules cannot physically fit. Performance-based checks—such as allowing compact units near a regulatory threshold to demonstrate compliance through an independently verified, reachable layout for an intended household schedule—can supplement statutory area minimums without rewarding unworkable micro-shells. For designers and manufacturers, space-saving investment should target two-person dwellings of roughly 12–30 m² NIA, prioritizing reliable day–night state switching for couples and compact multi-state footprints for parent-and-child households. Moreover, standardized furniture kits should be sized by floor-area band rather than marketed as universal micro-living packages, as larger couple surfaces expand adequacy across 13–16 m² Hong Kong model shells while reducing capacity below 13 m², and downsized commuter kits do not improve upon standard catalogue pieces.

For housing and computational design researchers, our modular evaluation pipeline and negative surrogate findings highlight both transferability and the continuing need for explicit geometric search. Because plan rasterization, household schedules, furniture catalogues, constraint solving, and independent validation are decoupled, the framework transfers directly to housing stocks in other cities, alternative cultural schedules, or stricter accessibility rules such as 0.90 m wheelchair circulation (Johanes et al., 2026). Conversely, testing whether fast geometry-only models—including GraphSAGE over room-and-opening graphs—could predict solver outcomes without optimization yielded a clear negative result (Figure S4; Table S3): on held-out Swiss Dwellings sites, GraphSAGE Spearman rank correlations ranged from 0.087 to 0.661 for S0 FCI and 0.088 to 0.588 for S2 FCI, below the prespecified 0.80 ranking criterion, and fell below retained lower bounds in 23 of 33 bound-only equivalent-area cases (despite post-hoc S0 adequacy ROC AUCs of 0.796 for couples and 0.888 for parent and child). Discrete doorway keep-outs, orthogonal pose packing, and multi-slot reachability create sharp geometric thresholds that summary polygon features do not reliably approximate, making warm-started constraint optimization with exact validation essential.

### Limitations of the study

Three scope boundaries frame the interpretation of our results and point to future research. (1) FCI measures geometric fit and walkable access for scheduled furniture activities, not acoustic or visual privacy, daylight quality, thermal comfort, ceiling-height perception, or occupant satisfaction; future post-occupancy and immersive-evaluation studies should link FCI and transformation burden to resident wellbeing and observed daily switching behavior. (2) Our four primary households and audited furniture library represent stylized daily schedules and representative product dimensions rather than exhaustive population heterogeneity, although the open pipeline accepts custom time-use diaries, assistive-mobility clearances, and local furniture catalogues. (3) Our Hong Kong analysis evaluates a controlled grid of 8–20 m² model shells rather than weighted territory-wide stock totals because public census tables lack the joint distribution of household size and subdivided-unit floor area, motivating future linkage with measured subdivided-unit floor-plan surveys.
