On behalf of my co-authors, I am pleased to submit our Research Article, "**Translating furniture into floor area shows where compact homes gain space**", for consideration in *iScience*.

Cities are allowing homes to become smaller and regulate them mainly by floor area. Hong Kong now sets an 8 m² minimum for subdivided units, and recent proposals tie minimum space to household size. Transformable furniture, such as wall beds, fold-down desks and drop-leaf tables, is widely promoted as a way to make such homes work. Yet nobody has measured how much usable area this furniture actually saves across real homes and the daily routines of the people who live in them. Area rules do not consider furniture. Functional space standards rely on fixed, hand-drawn layouts. Design studies examine single rooms, and layout generators do not check whether a layout is physically usable throughout the day.

We address this gap by comparing each home with itself under three furniture strategies: static, transformable, and transformable and switched between times of day. Each strategy is given its best arrangement by constraint optimization, and every arrangement is checked by an independent geometric validator. In total we obtained 31,428 verified layouts for 2,619 compact floor plans and four household types. We then express the difference in the unit regulators use: the floor area a home with static furniture would need to work equally well.

The results show that the value of transformable furniture is real but conditional:

- Most observed plans already function with static furniture.
- Gains concentrate in two-person households in homes of intermediate compact size. The median identified equivalent-area gains are 6.21 m² for couples and 5.00 m² for parent-and-child households.
- Fixed 45 m² cutoffs disagree with modeled adequacy for about half of two-person cases, mostly by rejecting homes that work.
- In Hong Kong model shells, switching furniture between times of day is worth 2.24 m² for couples. The best furniture dimensions depend on the target size band.

We believe the work suits *iScience* because it is interdisciplinary by design. It combines optimization methods from computer science with architecture and interior design, and it applies them to a pressing urban housing question. It extends functional space standards with time and transformability. It tests single-room design claims at the scale of housing stocks, and it complements generative layout methods by guaranteeing that every layout is feasible and reachable. The framework is transferable to other cities, households and furniture catalogues. All analyzed datasets are public, and the code and derived results are openly available at https://github.com/Casey-sketch-cyber/research.

This manuscript is original, has not been published previously, and is not under consideration elsewhere. All authors have approved the submission and declare no competing interests.
