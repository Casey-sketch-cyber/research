# Evaluating Transformable Furniture in Compact Housing: Schedule-Aware Equivalent Floor Area and Implications for Minimum Space Standards

**Authors:** Feng He ¹, Dan Zhang ² and Zhantu Liang ³,*

¹ Faculty of Art and Design, Guangxi Polytechnic University, Nanning 530226, China
² School of Art and Design, Guangzhou Polytechnic University, Guangzhou 511483, China
³ Institute of Ultrasonic Technology, Shenzhen Polytechnic University, Shenzhen 518055, China
\* Correspondence: liangzhantu@szpu.edu.cn

**Abstract:** Compact urban dwellings are regulated primarily by floor area, while transformable furniture is widely promoted to expand livable space. Yet area-only rules, single-room prototypes, and layout generators cannot establish how much usable floor area transformable furniture saves across diverse homes and daily routines. Here, we compare each dwelling against itself under static (S0), form-only (S1), and form + time (S2) furniture strategies—arranged by constraint optimization and independently verified—and translate capacity differences into equivalent floor area (m²). Across 2619 compact plans and four household schedules (31,428 verified layouts), most observed plans (61.0%–90.8%) already function with static furniture. Gains concentrate in two-person households at intermediate compact sizes, with median identified positive equivalent-area gains of 6.21 m² for couples and 5.00 m² for parent-and-child households, driven by compact form and daily switching for couples but mainly by form for parent and child. Fixed 45 m² cutoffs disagree with modeled S2 adequacy on 55.7% of couple and 52.7% of parent-and-child observed cases, while daily switching saves 2.24 m² for couples in Hong Kong model shells. Transformable furniture's value is therefore conditional, supporting performance-based supplements to area rules and size-band furniture design.

**Keywords:** compact housing; minimum space standards; transformable furniture; functional capacity; layout optimization; equivalent area; housing regulation

## 1. Introduction

Urban housing pressure and rising construction costs are shrinking residential floor areas across dense cities, yet public policy still regulates compact dwellings primarily by internal floor area alone. Hong Kong's Basic Housing Units Ordinance, for instance, establishes a statutory minimum internal floor area of 8 m² for subdivided units, mostly commencing on 1 March 2026 [@hksar2025basic], whereas normative housing scholarship on human flourishing proposes 30, 45, and 60 m² for one-, two-, and three-person households [@ulrich2025minimum]. How much floor area a household requires also carries substantial environmental stakes: effective space utilization in existing residential buildings can reduce operational and embodied carbon more than envelope retrofits across many buildings in dense cities [@weber2024hypergraph]. Within this dual pressure of affordability and decarbonization, transformable furniture—such as wall beds, fold-down desks, and drop-leaf tables—is widely promoted by architects and developers to expand domestic functionality without enlarging physical building footprints [@goessler2023adaptive; @daglio2023housing].

Despite these claims, existing housing regulations and functional space standards cannot establish how much usable floor area transformable furniture saves across diverse dwellings and households. Area-only rules count square meters of net internal area (NIA) without testing whether beds, tables, storage, and unobstructed walking paths actually fit inside a specific room plan; as empirical floor-plan analyses demonstrate, floor area alone is an incomplete proxy for spatial usability [@ozer2022dwelling]. Functional space standards and architectural layout audits improve upon bare area thresholds by checking rooms against furniture lists and dimensional indicators [@pedro2009dwellings; @qaradaghi2022area; @freimane2022riga; @rakhmatillaeva2025spatial; @ulrich2025minimum]. However, those methods evaluate static furniture in fixed, hand-drawn arrangements and omit time-of-day space sharing across a household's daily routine, leaving them unable to assess multi-state furniture or quantify its floor-area equivalence.

Architectural studies of transformable interiors leave a second, complementary gap. Prototype analyses, room-level ergonomic models, and user surveys report large localized benefits—including roughly 60% more free activity area in a 12 m² bedroom when a wall bed folds up [@bu2026compact], increased open floor area in a modeled 35 m² apartment [@elmurodov2026efficiency], room-depth compression under ergonomic action domains [@zeng2026sbf], and user-reported flexibility in compact residential layouts [@ashkevari2023flexibility; @daglio2023housing]. Because these studies evaluate isolated rooms or designer-selected case studies, they conflate furniture capability with manual layout drawing skill and cannot distinguish where transformability resolves genuine spatial bottlenecks across heterogeneous housing stocks from where static layouts already suffice or room geometry blocks transformation altogether.

Computational interior layout research addresses automated placement but leaves the housing-scale measurement problem unresolved. Existing methods synthesize static or ergonomics-guided residential scenes [@merrell2011furniture; @yu2011makeithome; @sun2025layoutvlm; @li2026homeworld; @nieciecki2026comfortable; @xiang2026colayout] or design reconfigurable furniture mechanisms and multi-state transitions [@garg2016reconfigurables; @xiong2021motion; @fu2024magic; @liu2025diff; @zhang2025sceneexplorer; @xiong2026mafc]. Yet none ties fixed-anchor furniture states to a 24-hour household activity schedule, independently guarantees collision-free operating clearances and continuous walkable reachability from the dwelling entrance across every time slot, and translates resulting capacity differences into floor area (m²) across large corpora of compact plans. Across space standards, architectural case studies, and computational layout tools, the root cause of this evidence gap is the same: no existing method jointly represents who occupies a dwelling and when, which furniture items can change state at fixed positions, and whether every scheduled arrangement is collision-free and reachable, expressed in the square-meter currency of housing policy.

To close this gap, we develop a schedule-aware, independently verifiable evaluation framework that compares each compact dwelling against itself under three nested furniture strategies: static (S0), form only (S1, admitting transformable furniture in a single constant daily state), and form + time (S2, allowing transformable furniture at fixed anchor positions to switch states across daily time slots). Giving every strategy its best constraint-optimized arrangement inside the same physical shell removes manual design bias, while an independent geometric validator verifies non-overlap, directional operating clearances, and 0.60 m continuous walkable access in every slot. Measuring the share of scheduled, weighted activity-hours served yields a functional capacity index (FCI); mapping paired S2 versus S0 scores onto the monotone static area–capacity curve translates capacity differences into equivalent-area gain ($\Delta\text{EA}$, in m²) and minimum adequate area ($A_{\min}$ at $\text{FCI} \ge 0.95$). Decomposing total gains into the form effect ($\text{S1} - \text{S0}$) and the time or switching effect ($\text{S2} - \text{S1}$) further separates compact product geometry from daily temporal reuse.

Using 2619 compact dwellings (2109 observed plans and 510 controlled generated shells) evaluated across four household schedules—producing 31,428 independently verified layouts—this study answers three research questions (RQ1–RQ3):

- **RQ1:** Across heterogeneous compact dwellings and daily household routines, how much equivalent floor area ($\Delta\text{EA}$ and $\Delta A_{\min}$) does transformable furniture save relative to optimized static layouts, and how is that gain split between compact product form and daily state switching?
- **RQ2:** How accurately do fixed statutory and normative floor-area cutoffs classify modeled functional adequacy when interior dwelling geometry varies at the same floor area?
- **RQ3:** Within ultra-compact housing bands such as Hong Kong's 8–20 m² subdivided-unit policy regime, how do minimum area thresholds, daily state switching, and parametric furniture dimensions interact?

Our study makes three contributions: (1) a schedule-aware, independently verifiable measure of residential functional capacity (FCI) translatable into equivalent floor area ($\Delta\text{EA}$) and minimum adequate area ($A_{\min}$); (2) corpus-scale paired evidence pinpointing where transformable form and daily state switching save floor area, why both the smallest and largest compact dwellings gain little, and how single-room free-area claims relate to whole-dwelling performance; and (3) an empirical evaluation of area-only housing standards together with a Hong Kong model-shell scenario showing that compact furniture dimensions should be chosen by floor-area band. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 details the dwelling corpus, schedules, furniture catalogue, optimization, independent validation, and statistical methods; Section 3 presents the empirical findings across observed plans, generated shells, area comparators, and Hong Kong model shells; Section 4 interprets these results for architectural design, housing regulation, and computational layout research alongside study limitations; and Section 5 summarizes the main conclusions.

## 2. Materials and Methods

### 2.1. Overview of the Framework

To quantify how much usable floor area transformable furniture adds without confounding product capability with manual layout drawing, each compact dwelling is compared against itself under S0, S1, and S2 using automated constraint optimization paired with an independent geometric and reachability validator (Figure 1). The workflow comprises eleven modular stages: (1) floor-plan normalization, NIA measurement, and empty-geometry evaluability screening (Section 2.2); (2) 24-hour household activity schedules (Section 2.3); (3) an audited catalogue of static and two-state transformable furniture with explicit operating clearances (Section 2.4); (4) within-dwelling strategy definitions separating compact product form from daily state switching (Section 2.4); (5) discrete candidate pose generation and warm-started constraint-programming optimization (Section 2.5); (6) zero-shared-code validation of collisions, clearances, and continuous walkable access (Section 2.6); (7) schedule-weighted scoring of FCI and daily transformation burden (TB) (Section 2.7); (8) monotone static reference-curve inversion for $\Delta\text{EA}$ and support bounds (Section 2.8); (9) logistic crossing estimation for $A_{\min}$ (Section 2.8); (10) solver benchmarking, area-cutoff comparisons, sensitivity analyses, room-level free-area comparisons, geometry-only surrogate testing, parametric furniture kit sizing, and the Hong Kong subdivided-unit scenario (Section 2.9); and (11) paired unit- and template-cluster bootstrap inference (Section 2.10). Software versions and public code availability are summarized in Section 2.11.

### 2.2. Dwelling Corpus and Net Internal Area

Comparing housing regulations and furniture strategies across heterogeneous floor-plan collections requires a uniform interior area definition and a prior check that unfurnished plans are physically traversable. We assembled compact dwellings within an inclusion band of 8–50 m² of NIA from two public repositories of observed floor plans—Swiss Dwellings v3.0.0 (D1) [@standfest2022swiss] and ResPlan (D2) [@abouagour2025resplan]—alongside a controlled parametric grid of generated shells (D3; Table 1; Figure S1). NIA is defined as the union of four interior zone classes: furnishable space (bedrooms, living rooms, dining rooms, studies, studios, and general habitable rooms), storage-only space (cloakrooms and storerooms), walkable-only circulation and kitchen circulation space (corridors, halls, foyers, lobbies, and kitchen or kitchen-dining zones), and fixed-service space (bathrooms, sanitary rooms, and wash/dry rooms). Structural walls, balconies, loggias, shafts, staircases, basements, and exterior areas are excluded. Plan eligibility required residential usage, at least one habitable furnishable room, an entrance door, at least one window segment, sanitary provision, and kitchen provision.

Each raw plan was normalized to metric coordinates, cleaned by closing minor vectorization gaps up to 0.15 m, rotated to align with its dominant wall axis, and deduplicated within each data source using normalized geometry hashes while preserving building-site identifiers. This process yielded 2327 unique eligible compact dwellings in D1 (from 46,830 raw records) and 884 unique eligible compact dwellings in D2 (from 17,000 raw records, retaining the corpus metric scale calibration). For D1, we drew a stratified sample across 2 m² NIA bins and room counts capped at 5 units per building site ($n = 1348$ selected), while all 884 eligible D2 units were retained. Because neither D1 nor D2 contained eligible observed plans smaller than 16.75 m² NIA, we generated 510 controlled shells (D3) spanning 17 target areas (8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 18, 20, 22, 25, 28, 32, 36, and 40 m² NIA) crossed with five aspect ratios (1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5, and 3.0), three entrance positions (short-wall center, short-wall corner, and long-wall corner), and two window positions (opposite short wall and long wall). Every generated shell contains a fixed $1.20 \times 1.50\text{ m}$ corner toilet pod and a $1.20 \times 0.60\text{ m}$ wall kitchenette near the entrance, with a 2.50 m ceiling height. Throughout the primary analysis, observed plans ($\text{D1} + \text{D2}$) and generated shells (D3) are evaluated separately; combined curves are reported only as a pooled sensitivity analysis.

Before any furniture optimization, all 2742 selected plans were rasterized onto a 0.10 m grid and tested against a predeclared empty-geometry evaluability screen. To pass, an unfurnished dwelling had to provide continuous 0.60 m walkable access from the entrance to every fixed-service target and leave at least half of its furnishable raster cells either inside the 0.60 m walking region or reachable within an additional 0.60 m across unobstructed cells without crossing walls or diagonal corners. Exactly 123 observed units (112 in D1 and 11 in D2, typically caused by narrow or oblique doorway vectorizations at 0.10 m resolution) failed this unfurnished screen and were excluded altogether. The final evaluable corpus therefore comprises 2619 dwellings: 1236 in D1 (NIA 16.75–50.00 m², median 40.47 m²), 873 in D2 (NIA 25.56–49.96 m², median 45.48 m²; 2109 observed plans in total), and 510 in D3 (NIA 8.00–40.00 m², median 16.00 m²; Table 1; Figure S1). Whenever no furniture item could be feasibly placed inside an evaluable dwelling, its independently validated empty layout was retained with $\text{FCI} = 0$ (94 layouts across the primary experiment), avoiding survivor bias in ultra-small footprints.

### 2.3. Household Activity Schedules

Conventional static furniture checklists test whether items fit simultaneously, ignoring whether household members require different activities at different hours or concurrent surfaces within the same hour. We therefore model domestic demand over six contiguous time slots summing to 24 h: night (23:00–07:00, 8 h), morning (07:00–09:00, 2 h), day (09:00–17:00, 8 h), evening (17:00–19:00, 2 h), dinner (19:00–20:00, 1 h), and leisure (20:00–23:00, 3 h; Table 2). Every evaluable dwelling is assessed against four primary household schedules:

1. **Single commuter (1 person):** away during the 8 h day slot; requires single sleeping at night, two-person dining capacity in morning and dinner, 1.0 storage unit, one-person lounging in evening and leisure, and optional individual desk work during leisure weighted at 0.3.
2. **Single remote worker (1 person):** identical to the single commuter except for adding an 8 h daytime individual work requirement and removing the optional leisure work demand.
3. **Couple (2 persons, one remote worker):** requires double-occupancy sleeping at night (satisfiable by either one double sleeping surface or two single sleeping surfaces), 2.0 storage units, daytime individual work, two-person lounging in evening and leisure, and two day variants: normal days at frequency 0.857 requiring two-person dining at dinner, and guest-dinner days at frequency 0.143 requiring four-person dining at dinner.
4. **Parent and child (2 persons):** away during the day; requires two separate single sleeping surfaces at night, 1.5 storage units, simultaneous individual work and one-person lounging during evening, two-person dining in morning and dinner, and two-person lounging during leisure.

A fifth schedule representing an older adult staying home by day under widened 0.90 m walking width and $1.2\times$ operating clearances is evaluated in sensitivity analyses (Table 2). Hygiene and cooking are served by the required fixed bathroom and kitchen zones, whose doorways and service targets constrain walkable circulation in every slot. Within any slot, simultaneous non-storage activity demands must be served by distinct, simultaneously reachable furniture instances; storage demand receives proportional credit $\min(1, \text{reachable placed capacity} / \text{required units})$.

### 2.4. Furniture Library, Clearances, and the Three Strategies

Fair evaluation of transformability requires explicit, realistic footprints and directional user clearances for every static and two-state item. The audited furniture library contains nine static item types (single bed, double bed, wardrobe, shelving, desk, two-person dining table, four-person dining table, two-seat sofa, and armchair) and seven two-state transformable item types (single wall bed, double wall bed, wall bed over sofa, sofa bed, drop-leaf table, fold-down desk, and extendable dining table; Table S1). Each item state specifies a rigid rectangular body footprint (width along the wall by depth into the room), directional operating clearance strips (front, back, left, right), wall-backing rules, height class (tall items may not back onto window segments), storage capacity units (1.0 for a standard wardrobe, 0.5 for shelving), per-dwelling maximum instance counts (up to 2 for single beds, single wall beds, wardrobes, shelving, armchairs, and desks—ensuring two-sleeper households can place two single beds or two single wall beds—and 1 otherwise), and per-transition effort weights (0.2 for fold-down desks and drop-leaf tables, 0.3 for extendable tables, 1.0 for single and double wall beds, 1.2 for wall beds over sofas, and 1.5 for sofa beds; Table S1). Catalogue dimensions were audited against 43,002 usable product dimension triples extracted from 147,702 Amazon Berkeley Objects (ABO) listings (D4) [@collins2022abo]. Furniture bodies may never overlap walls, fixed wet services, door-swing keep-out squares (width $\times$ width on the room side, or both sides where swing direction is ambiguous), or other active furniture bodies. Clearance zones may overlap walkable floor space and other items' clearances, but never another active item's body.

To separate the geometric benefit of compact transformable product forms from the temporal benefit of reconfiguring rooms across daily time slots, every dwelling–household pair is evaluated under three nested furniture strategies:

1. **Static (S0):** admits only the nine static furniture types in fixed poses.
2. **Form only (S1):** admits both static and transformable furniture types, but constrains each placed item to a single constant state across all six slots and day variants, capturing compact product footprints without daily switching.
3. **Form + time (S2):** admits static and transformable furniture at fixed anchor coordinates and orientations across the day and across day variants, while allowing each transformable item to switch between its stowed and deployed states across the six daily slots (and between normal and guest days).

Under this nested design, $\text{S1} - \text{S0}$ isolates the **form effect**, $\text{S2} - \text{S1}$ isolates the **time effect** (or **switching effect**), and $\text{S2} - \text{S0}$ measures the total transformable gain. Across 2619 evaluable dwellings, four primary households, and three strategies, the pipeline produces 31,428 independently verified layouts.

### 2.5. Schedule-Constrained Layout Optimization

Discrete furniture packing under doorway keep-outs, directional clearances, and multi-slot pedestrian connectivity is combinatorial; hand-drawn layouts risk understating one strategy relative to another. For every dwelling, candidate anchor poses are generated across the four orthogonal orientations ($0^\circ, 90^\circ, 180^\circ, 270^\circ$) on the 0.10 m collision grid, discarding poses whose bodies, clearances, wall-backing strips, or tall-item window rules violate empty-shell boundaries. In the accepted production configuration, dwellings with $\text{NIA} \le 20\text{ m}^2$ use a 0.10 m candidate anchor stride and a 120 s wall-clock solver cap per strategy run, whereas dwellings with $\text{NIA} > 20\text{ m}^2$ use a 0.20 m candidate anchor stride (while retaining the 0.10 m collision and walkability raster) and a 30 s wall-clock cap.

Each dwelling–household–strategy problem is formulated as a constraint-programming model and solved with the CP-SAT (constraint programming–Boolean satisfiability) solver of Google OR-Tools [@perron2023cpsat] using boolean pose-selection, per-slot state-selection, and slot-demand assignment variables subject to pairwise body–body and body–clearance conflict constraints. The solver maximizes a lexicographic-style weighted objective that prioritizes served weighted activity-hours (weight 1000.0), penalizes cyclic transformation burden (weight $-1.0$), and applies a minor tie-breaking preference for window proximity (weight 0.01). To guarantee continuous walkable access without encoding full grid-flow constraints upfront, each run starts from a deterministic greedy feasible layout—and, for S1 and S2 in the production pipeline, may warm-start from the revalidated preceding-strategy solution on the same dwelling and household, ensuring monotonic warm-start availability without relaxing independent validation. CP-SAT runs in rounds of up to 5.0 s (up to 30 lazy-constraint iterations); each incumbent is checked with the exact reachability test, nogood cuts are added to exclude unreachable pose–state combinations when needed, and the highest-scoring independently verified incumbent is retained.

### 2.6. Independent Geometric and Reachability Validator

Optimization models can harbor subtle discretization or constraint-encoding defects if evaluated solely by their internal objective. Every layout reported in this paper therefore passes an independent geometric and reachability validator that shares zero code or data structures with candidate generation or CP-SAT modeling. Reading only the raw configuration files and output item coordinates $(x, y, \theta)$ plus slot states, the validator independently reconstructs outward-rounded 0.10 m raster masks for every furniture body, operating clearance strip, and wall-backing strip, and verifies five conditions: (1) strategy eligibility and per-type instance caps; (2) full containment of non-storage bodies in furnishable cells, storage bodies in furnishable or storage-only cells, and clearances in furnishable, storage-only, or walkable cells; (3) wall contact for wall-backed items and zero window overlap for tall wall-backed items; (4) zero body–body overlap and zero clearance–body collision in every slot and day variant; and (5) continuous 0.60 m walkable reachability from the entrance to every fixed-service target and to at least one clearance cell of every active furniture item credited with serving a scheduled activity.

Walkable reachability is evaluated via explicit disc erosion of diameter 0.60 m on unobstructed walkable cells, four-neighbor breadth-first search from the entrance extension, and matching disc dilation. Unreachable items receive zero service credit, and any structural boundary violation or loss of fixed-service access invalidates the layout. As an additional three-dimensional (3D) visual check, 10 validated scenes across five showcase dwellings were exported to Blender 4.5.3 LTS (Cycles renderer) to generate 30 orthographic stills ($2400 \times 1800\text{ px}$), and projected 3D mesh outlines were audited against two-dimensional (2D) validator footprints across 17 placed furniture instances; the maximum Hausdorff outline discrepancy was 2.79 mm, well below the 20 mm tolerance.

### 2.7. Functional Capacity Index and Transformation Burden

To compare diverse household schedules on a common $[0, 1]$ scale, let $V$ denote a household's day variants with occurrence frequencies $p_v$ ($\sum_{v \in V} p_v = 1$), $S$ the six daily time slots with durations $h_s$ (hours), and $D_{v,s}$ the set of furniture-supported activity demands $d$ in variant $v$ and slot $s$ with importance weights $w_d$ ($w_d = 1.0$ unless stated otherwise). Given validator-verified service credits $c_d \in [0, 1]$ ($c_d \in \{0, 1\}$ for discrete activities and $c_d = \min(1, U_{\text{placed}} / U_{\text{req}})$ for storage), the variant functional capacity index is

$$\text{FCI}_v = \frac{\sum_{s \in S} h_s \sum_{d \in D_{v,s}} w_d \, c_d}{\sum_{s \in S} h_s \sum_{d \in D_{v,s}} w_d}$$

and the household functional capacity index is $\text{FCI} = \sum_{v \in V} p_v \, \text{FCI}_v$. By design, FCI measures physical furniture fit and walkable reachability for scheduled domestic activities, not acoustic or visual privacy, thermal or daylight quality, ceiling-height perception, or subjective comfort.

Daily transformation burden (TB) aggregates effort-weighted cyclic state transitions across all placed furniture items $i \in I$ over the six consecutive slots, including the wrap-around transition from leisure back to night ($s_0 \equiv s_6$):

$$\text{TB} = \sum_{v \in V} p_v \sum_{i \in I} e_i \sum_{k=1}^{6} \mathbb{I}\!\left[\sigma_{i,v,s_k} \neq \sigma_{i,v,s_{k-1}}\right]$$

where $e_i$ is item $i$'s effort weight and $\sigma_{i,v,s_k}$ is its state in slot $s_k$.

### 2.8. Equivalent-Area Gain and Minimum Adequate Area

Translating functional capacity differences into square meters expresses furniture performance in the unit used by building regulations and residential design standards. For each household and reference population (observed plans $\text{D1} + \text{D2}$, generated shells D3, individual observed datasets, or the pooled sensitivity sample), dwellings are grouped into 2 m² NIA bins $[8, 10), [10, 12), \dots, [48, 50]$. At each bin midpoint $x_b$, we compute the median validated static score $\tilde{y}_b = \text{median}[\text{FCI}(\text{S0})]$, enforce non-decreasing static capacity with area via a cumulative maximum $y_b = \max_{j \le b} \tilde{y}_j$, and linearly interpolate between midpoints to construct the continuous monotone static reference curve $\hat{f}_0(A)$ over supported midpoints $[A_{\text{lo}}, A_{\text{hi}}]$.

For any matched dwelling–household pair $u$ with validated scores $y_0 = \text{FCI}_u(\text{S0})$ and $y_2 = \text{FCI}_u(\text{S2})$, both scores are inverted onto $\hat{f}_0$ at the smallest supported area attaining each level: $A^*(y) = \inf \{A \in [A_{\text{lo}}, A_{\text{hi}}] : \hat{f}_0(A) \ge y\}$. Avoiding parametric extrapolation yields three identification cases:

1. **Exact zero:** if $y_2 = y_0$, then $\Delta\text{EA}_u = 0\text{ m}^2$ exactly.
2. **Point-identified gain:** if $y_2 > y_0$, $y_0 > \hat{f}_0(A_{\text{lo}})$ (so $A^*(y_0) > A_{\text{lo}}$), and $y_2 \le \hat{f}_0(A_{\text{hi}})$, both inverses lie strictly within interior curve support and $\Delta\text{EA}_u = A^*(y_2) - A^*(y_0)$ is point-identified.
3. **Support-bounded gain:** if $y_2 > y_0$ and either $A^*(y_0) = A_{\text{lo}}$ (left-boundary censoring, because a smaller shell below observed NIA support could already achieve $y_0$) or $y_2 > \hat{f}_0(A_{\text{hi}})$ (right-boundary censoring, where $A^*(y_2)$ is bounded below by $A_{\text{hi}}$ and unbounded above), the pair yields an identified lower bound $\Delta\text{EA}_u^{\text{lo}} = \max(0, A_{\text{lo or } *}(y_2) - A^*(y_0))$ rather than an extrapolated point value.

Accordingly, three complementary summaries are reported per household and population: (i) the median among point-identified positive gains, (ii) the support-valid lower bound on the median across all improved pairs ($y_2 > y_0$), and (iii) lower and upper support bounds on the all-pair median across every evaluated dwelling (including unchanged pairs). A pooled-reference sensitivity re-inverts observed-plan scores onto the combined $\text{D1} + \text{D2} + \text{D3}$ static curve extending down to 8 m² NIA.

To estimate the floor area at which half of dwellings satisfy a household's daily schedule, binary functional adequacy is defined at threshold $\tau = 0.95$ ($\text{FCI} \ge 0.95$, with sensitivity checks at $\tau = 0.90$ and $\tau = 1.00$). For each household, population, and strategy, we fit an unpenalized logistic regression $\Pr(\text{FCI} \ge \tau \mid \text{NIA} = A) = \text{logit}^{-1}(\beta_0 + \beta_1 A)$ and define the minimum adequate area $A_{\min} = -\beta_0 / \beta_1$ as the 50% adequacy crossing, giving the strategy reduction $\Delta A_{\min} = A_{\min}(\text{S0}) - A_{\min}(\text{S2})$. Crossings are reported only when $\beta_1 > 0$, both adequate and inadequate outcomes occur without complete separation, and $A_{\min}$ lies within the population's observed NIA range; otherwise the crossing is withheld as unsupported.

### 2.9. Sensitivity Analyses, Benchmarks, Kits, and the Hong Kong Scenario

Five targeted validation and application analyses complement the primary corpus study. First, to verify that search quality does not drive paired strategy differences, we froze a stratified benchmark of 200 dwelling–household pairs balanced across data source, area tercile, and household schedule, comparing standalone S2 CP-SAT, descending-footprint greedy wall-adjacent placement, and standalone simulated annealing (20,000 proposals $\times$ 3 restarts with a geometric cooling schedule initialized from standalone greedy) against the accepted warm-started production pipeline, re-scoring every layout with the independent validator.

Second, to test how well area-only rules proxy for modeled adequacy ($\text{FCI} \ge 0.95$), we measured within-bin FCI interquartile ranges (IQRs) across 2 m² NIA bins and computed confusion matrices—true positives (TP), true negatives (TN), false positives (FP), false negatives (FN), and total disagreement share $(\text{FP} + \text{FN}) / \text{Cases}$—against Ulrich's normative area cutoffs (30 m² for one-person schedules and 45 m² applied to both two-person schedules; Ulrich proposes 60 m² for three-person households) [@ulrich2025minimum] and Hong Kong's statutory 8 m² basic housing unit minimum internal floor area [@hksar2025basic], supplemented by continuous receiver-operating threshold sweeps on area per person and rooms per person.

Third, sensitivity analyses tested modeling assumptions across three cohorts: (i) drop-one removals of individual transformable items, global operating-clearance scaling ($0.8\times, 1.0\times, 1.2\times$), widened walking circulation (0.60 m versus 0.90 m), and the older-adult schedule on the 200 benchmark pairs; (ii) raster/anchor resolution (0.05, 0.10, and 0.20 m) and CP-SAT time limits (30, 60, and 120 s) on a 50-unit subsample; and (iii) a dedicated 348-unit small-dwelling comparison ($\text{NIA} \le 20\text{ m}^2$) allowing mirrored one-side access (0.60 m on one long side) for double beds, double wall beds, wall beds over sofas, and deployed sofa beds.

Fourth, to connect whole-dwelling findings to single-room literature and test whether fast learned models could replace optimization, we conducted two auxiliary evaluations. For the single-room comparison, we built an approximate $4 \times 3\text{ m}$ bedroom envelope modeled after Bu and Che Mat [@bu2026compact] plus three 12 m² generated shell analogues (aspect ratios 1.0, 1.25, and 1.5), comparing daytime body-free floor area (NIA minus placed daytime furniture bodies, retaining wet-service footprints where present) against Bu and Che Mat's clearance-deducted figures and across all single-person pairs in $\text{D1} + \text{D2}$ and D3. For the geometry-only surrogate test, D1 was split by building site into 70% training (884 units across 323 sites), 15% validation (180 units across 69 sites), and 15% held-out test groups (172 units across 70 sites), with D2 ($n = 873$) and D3 ($n = 510$) serving as external transfer sets. Four models—an area-only linear model, LightGBM gradient-boosted trees, a tabular multilayer perceptron (MLP), and a GraphSAGE graph neural network over room-polygon and opening adjacency graphs—predicted $\text{FCI}(\text{S0})$, $\text{FCI}(\text{S2})$, and D1-training-referenced $\Delta\text{EA}$ against a prespecified Spearman $\rho \ge 0.80$ criterion, alongside a post-hoc receiver operating characteristic area under the curve (ROC AUC) diagnostic on ranking $\text{FCI} \ge 0.95$.

Fifth, to evaluate furniture sizing in ultra-compact dwellings and examine Hong Kong's subdivided-unit policy regime, we formulated a dimension-sensitive quality index ($\text{FCI}_q$) weighting served activities by piecewise-linear dimensional quality factors $q \in [0.60, 1.00]$ across six design variables: single wall-bed width (0.80, 0.90, 1.00 m with $q = 0.60, 0.85, 1.00$), double wall-bed width (1.20, 1.40, 1.50 m with $q = 0.60, 0.90, 1.00$), fold-down desk width (0.80, 1.00, 1.20 m with $q = 0.70, 0.90, 1.00$) and deployed depth (0.45, 0.55 m with $q = 0.80, 1.00$), drop-leaf table width (0.70, 0.80, 1.00 m with $q = 0.80, 0.90, 1.00$) and deployed depth (0.60, 0.75 m with $q = 0.85, 1.00$), and wardrobe frontage (0.60, 1.00, 1.40 m providing 0.6, 1.0, 1.4 storage units), holding stowed depths and clearances fixed as in the main catalogue. Identical quality curves applied to standard catalogue items and candidate kits, with adequacy defined at $\text{FCI}_q \ge 0.95$. Candidate kits for the single commuter and couple were screened by successive halving and ranked by mean $\text{FCI}_q$ (TB breaking ties) on 30 frozen D3 training shells spanning 8–15 m², compared against a per-shell bespoke upper bound (selecting the best tested kit vector separately per shell), transferred to 15 held-out D3 shells (aspect ratio 2.5, long-wall-corner entrance) and 15 small observed plans ($\text{NIA} \le 20\text{ m}^2$), and verified under matched additional 120 s warm-started S2 search alongside the catalogue. Finally, we evaluated 390 Hong Kong model shells—30 matched aspect-ratio, entrance, and window templates at every integer NIA from 8 to 20 m²—for the single commuter, couple, and parent and child under standard strategies and selected kits using the shared $\text{FCI}_q \ge 0.95$ rule. Territory-wide census counts (108,202 subdivided units, 107,371 households, and 215,709 residents across 33,658 quarters; D6) were drawn from the Hong Kong 2021 Population Census (Table 1); because public tabulations omit the joint household-size-by-floor-area distribution, scenario results are reported directly on the 390-shell template grid without territory-wide stock weighting.

### 2.10. Statistical Analysis

All statistical summaries and uncertainty intervals were computed in Python using fixed random seed 20261105. Because S0, S1, and S2 (as well as alternative solvers and furniture kits) are evaluated on identical dwellings, all primary comparisons are paired within physical dwelling units. Two-sided 95% confidence intervals (CIs) use 1000 non-parametric paired bootstrap resamples. On D1, D2, and D3, the resampling unit is the physical dwelling stratified by data source, preserving all paired household and strategy evaluations within each resampled dwelling. In the 390-shell Hong Kong scenario, the resampling unit is the 30-shape geometric template cluster, resampling complete 8–20 m² area trajectories per template to preserve cross-area correlation. Within every bootstrap draw, static reference curves $\hat{f}_0(A)$, equivalent-area inversions, and unpenalized logistic $A_{\min}$ crossings are refitted from scratch before taking the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. CIs for area statistics ($\Delta\text{EA}$ medians and $A_{\min}$ or $\Delta A_{\min}$ crossings) are reported only when at least two distinct units contribute and at least 80% of the 1000 bootstrap draws yield finite within-support estimates; otherwise point estimates are reported without intervals or withheld as unsupported, and boundary-censored equivalent-area estimates are explicitly denoted with ≥ lower bounds or lower–upper support intervals.

## 3. Results

### 3.1. A Verified, Schedule-Aware Comparison of Furniture Strategies

Applying the empty-geometry walkability screen and multi-strategy pipeline yielded a large corpus of verified layouts spanning observed compact apartments down to micro-scale shells (Section 2.2; Table 1; Figure S1). Of the 2742 selected dwellings, 2619 passed the unfurnished walkability screen, and the generated shells extended coverage below the smallest observed plan down to Hong Kong's 8 m² statutory minimum. Evaluating every dwelling across the four household schedules and three furniture strategies produced 31,428 independently verified layouts (Sections 2.3–2.6; Table 2; Table S1).

Tracking individual homes across Night, Workday, and Dinner slots shows that interior geometry—not floor area alone—governs whether daily state switching unlocks usable capacity (Figure 2; Figure S2). In an 18.00 m² generated shell occupied by a couple, neither static (S0) nor form-only (S1) arrangements can fit a double sleeping surface, daytime workspace, lounging, and dining clearances simultaneously without collision (Figure 2). By contrast, in a 10.00 m² generated shell no double sleeping surface fits with valid clearance in any slot, and in a partitioned 29.97 m² Swiss Dwellings plan narrow rooms and doorway keep-outs block multi-state gains; in both dwellings, S2 furniture states remain fixed across the day (exact FCI and TB values in Figure 2 and Figure S2).

### 3.3. Gains Concentrate in Two-Person Households at Intermediate Sizes

Allowing transformable form and daily state switching (S2 versus S0) improves capacity mainly in two-person households rather than single-occupant dwellings (Figure 3; Figure 4; Table 3). On observed plans, S2 improves FCI in 7.5% of single-commuter and 8.7% of single-remote-worker cases. Improvement is far more frequent for two-person schedules, reaching 23.5% for couples and 35.9% for parent-and-child households (Table 3). Among improved pairs inverting within interior static-curve support (Section 2.8), median identified positive $\Delta\text{EA}$ is 1.33 m² (95% CI [0.75, 3.11]) for the single remote worker. Two-person gains are substantially larger, reaching 6.21 m² ([4.20, 10.00]) for couples and 5.00 m² ([4.20, 8.00]) for parent-and-child households (sample counts and support bounds in Table 3). For the single commuter, all improved observed-plan pairs sit at the lower support boundary, yielding only a support-limited lower bound (Table 3); similarly, sparse smallest-bin couple pairs are unbounded on the observed reference curve but finite once pooled with generated shells (Figure 4).

### 3.4. Changing Form and Changing over Time Help Different Households

Decomposing the total functional gain into the form effect ($\text{S1} - \text{S0}$) and the time or switching effect ($\text{S2} - \text{S1}$; Section 2.4) shows that households benefit through distinct physical mechanisms (Figure 3; Table 3). On observed plans, single-person improvements come almost exclusively from compact transformable form rather than slot-to-slot switching (Table 3). Between the two-person households, however, the physical mechanisms diverge sharply. For the parent-and-child schedule, compact footprints account for nearly the entire gain, with an observed mean form effect of 0.0271 FCI versus a mean time effect of 0.0017 FCI (Table 3). For the couple, by contrast, form (0.0130 FCI) and daily switching (0.0112 FCI) contribute almost equally on observed plans—and both remain substantial in generated shells (Table 3)—because stowing a double wall bed by day directly frees floor space for daytime remote work, dining, and two-person lounging.

Sensitivity analyses indicate that this mechanistic split is not driven by bed-clearance conventions or solver settings, while exposing acute sensitivity to walking circulation width (Section 2.9; Figure S4). Allowing mirrored one-side access on double sleeping surfaces in small dwellings shifted the couple's mean time effect by only $-0.0122$ FCI, with confidence intervals including zero across small observed plans. Global clearance scaling altered mean S2 FCI by at most $-0.0160$, and varying solver time caps altered it by at most $-0.0102$ (Figure S4). In single-item drop-one tests, removing the drop-leaf table caused the largest capacity loss, reducing mean S2 FCI by $-0.0267$ for couples and $-0.0199$ for parent-and-child households (Figure S4). Widening required walking circulation from 0.60 m to 0.90 m, however, reduced mean S2 FCI by 0.5151 for the parent-and-child schedule (Figure S4), demonstrating that accessibility-grade circulation demands substantially larger base shells than standard passage rules.

### 3.5. Floor Area Alone Misjudges Functional Adequacy

Fixed statutory and normative floor-area cutoffs frequently misclassify modeled functional adequacy in compact housing (Section 2.9; Figure 5; Table 4). Across observed plans under S2, Ulrich's [@ulrich2025minimum] 45 m² two-person benchmark disagrees with modeled adequacy on 55.7% of couple cases and 52.7% of parent-and-child cases. Nearly all of these disagreements are false negatives—workable dwellings smaller than the threshold that satisfy scheduled daily activities with verified clearances and walkable access. Disagreement is already substantial under static furniture and widens further under S2 as transformable layouts make additional compact plans adequate, whereas Ulrich's one-person benchmark disagrees far less often on observed single-occupant cases (full confusion counts and S0 comparisons in Table 4).

### 3.6. Implications for Hong Kong's Smallest Homes

In ultra-compact Hong Kong model shells evaluated under the shared dimension-sensitive quality index (Section 2.9; Figure 6; Table S3), transformable furniture lowers the floor area each household needs, and for couples daily state switching itself accounts for a measurable share of that reduction. Because public census tabulations omit the joint household-size-by-floor-area distribution (Section 2.9; Table 1), these scenario estimates describe the 390 modeled shell templates directly. With standard catalogue dimensions, moving from S0 to S2 lowers minimum adequate area $A_{\min}$ from 13.40 m² to 12.37 m² for a single commuter, and from 19.58 m² to 17.07 m² for a parent and child—a reduction of 2.51 m² (Figure 6). For a couple, static furniture leaves $A_{\min}$ above the 20 m² grid support, whereas moving from form-only (S1) to form + time (S2) lowers $A_{\min}$ from 18.57 m² to 16.33 m², isolating a daily state-switching reduction of 2.24 m² (95% CI [1.66, 2.82]; Figure 6).

Parametric kit testing shows that downsizing furniture dimensions fails to improve upon standard pieces for a single commuter (Section 2.9; Figure 6; Figure S5; Table S3). Across the training shells, the highest-ranking compact commuter kit did not meaningfully outperform the standard catalogue on mean $\text{FCI}_q$ and achieved adequacy on fewer shells (dimensions, paired differences, and transfer cohorts in Figure S5 and Table S3). Across the Hong Kong model shells, it reached adequacy on fewer shells than the optimized catalogue (184 versus 246 of 390) because downsized sleeping, desk, and dining surfaces incur quality penalties without unlocking enough ultra-small shells (Figure 6; Table S3).

For a couple, by contrast, a larger parametric kit creates a clear size-band trade-off, demonstrating that furniture dimensions should be chosen per floor-area band rather than fixed across all compact homes (Figure 6; Figure S5; Table S3). On the training shells under matched search, paired mean $\text{FCI}_q$ differences over the catalogue had confidence intervals spanning zero, though adequacy edged higher (Table S3). Across the Hong Kong model shells, the larger kit lowered modeled $A_{\min}$ to 15.39 m² versus 16.12 m² for the optimized catalogue by expanding adequacy across the 13–16 m² band (51 versus 30 of 120 shells), yet it achieved zero adequacy below 13 m² and lowered mean $\text{FCI}_q$ in the smallest shells (exact band counts, dimensions, and confidence intervals in Figure 6 and Table S3).

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1. Interpretation Against Prior Work

Across our compact housing corpus and four daily household schedules, the space-saving value of transformable furniture is substantial where static arrangements hit geometric bottlenecks, yet conditional on household composition and dwelling size (Sections 3.2 and 3.3). Most observed compact plans already satisfy full scheduled demand with static furniture (Table 3), concentrating equivalent-area gains in two-person households of intermediate compact size—driven mainly by compact product form for a parent and child and by nearly equal contributions of form and daily switching for a couple (Section 3.4)—while ultra-small shells lack room even for stowed or deployed transformable clearances (Figure 2; Figure 4). Simultaneously, equal-area dwellings display wide dispersion in verified capacity (Figure 5), causing fixed area cutoffs to misclassify roughly half of observed two-person cases (Section 3.5; Table 4). Floor area alone is therefore an unreliable proxy for whether a compact dwelling works, whereas schedule-aware geometric verification isolates where furniture substitutes for space and where physical footprint remains binding.

These corpus-scale results clarify how large localized gains reported in single-room design studies relate to whole-dwelling capacity across housing stocks. Prototype and analytical studies of individual rooms—such as Bu and Che Mat's [@bu2026compact] bedroom comparison reporting roughly 60% more clearance-inclusive free activity area when a wall bed folds up, open-floor apartment comparisons [@elmurodov2026efficiency], and case-study projections of adaptive space efficiency [@goessler2023adaptive]—measure open floor area under a chosen room arrangement. Evaluating an approximate $4 \times 3\text{ m}$ bedroom envelope and matched 12 m² shell analogues under scheduled single-person demand and whole-home body-free accounting (Section 2.9; Figure S6) yields 7.60 m² of daytime body-free area under both S0 and S2 in the approximate room—because static placement already satisfies the full schedule without forced folding—a $-0.52$ to $+1.38\text{ m}^2$ range across the analogues, and a median daytime body-free area change of 0 m² across all observed plans and generated shells for both single-person schedules. Rather than contradicting single-room prototypes, this divergence reflects measure and scope: maximizing open daytime floor space in one room answers a different question than whether a whole dwelling's sleeping, work, dining, lounging, storage, cooking, and sanitary needs are jointly reachable across the day. Coupling multi-state furniture and daily schedules with independent access checks thus extends static functional space standards [@pedro2009dwellings; @ulrich2025minimum], empirical floor-plan usability audits [@ozer2022dwelling; @freimane2022riga; @qaradaghi2022area; @rakhmatillaeva2025spatial], and ergonomic depth-compression models [@zeng2026sbf], while complementing Weber et al.'s [@weber2024hypergraph] static excess-area carbon framework.

Our computational results also refine expectations for automated residential layout methods. Whereas existing computational interior tools synthesize static or ergonomics-guided layouts [@merrell2011furniture; @yu2011makeithome; @sun2025layoutvlm; @li2026homeworld; @nieciecki2026comfortable; @xiang2026colayout] or model reconfigurable assemblies and transitions [@garg2016reconfigurables; @xiong2021motion; @fu2024magic; @liu2025diff; @zhang2025sceneexplorer; @xiong2026mafc], pairing schedule-constrained CP-SAT optimization with a zero-shared-code reachability validator scales verified multi-slot comparison across thousands of heterogeneous plans. At the same time, our geometry-only surrogate evaluation (Section 2.9; Figure S7; Table S4) provides a cautionary benchmark for fast data-driven screening: none of the four geometry-only models—including GraphSAGE trained on room-polygon and opening adjacency graphs—met the prespecified Spearman $\rho \ge 0.80$ ranking criterion on held-out Swiss Dwellings sites, and most bound-only equivalent-area predictions violated retained lower bounds despite moderate post-hoc adequacy discrimination (full accuracy metrics in Figure S7 and Table S4). Doorway keep-outs, discrete pose packing, and multi-slot pedestrian reachability create sharp geometric thresholds that polygon summary graphs fail to capture reliably, confirming that explicit optimization coupled with independent validation remains necessary.

### 4.2. Implications for Architectural Design and Housing Regulation

Translating schedule-weighted functional capacity into square meters ($\Delta\text{EA}$ and $A_{\min}$) offers concrete operational guidance for housing regulators, architects, developers, and furniture manufacturers. For housing regulators, fixed floor-area minimums confront a sharp two-sided classification dilemma (Section 3.5; Table 4). Generous normative cutoffs such as Ulrich's [@ulrich2025minimum] 45 m² two-person threshold reject more than half of observed two-person cases under S2—overwhelmingly workable compact plans that satisfy scheduled activities with verified clearances and access. Conversely, ultra-low statutory floors such as Hong Kong's 8 m² basic housing unit minimum [@hksar2025basic] pass substantial shares of ultra-small shells in which basic daily routines cannot physically fit. Rather than abandoning minimum area standards, regulators can pair statutory floors with a performance-based compliance option: compact units near a regulatory threshold could qualify by submitting an independently verified, collision-free, reachable multi-slot layout for an explicit target occupancy, while $\Delta A_{\min}$ estimates—such as the 2.24 m² couple switching reduction in Hong Kong model shells (Section 3.6)—bound how much floor-area flexibility transformable fit-outs legitimately justify.

For architects and residential developers, the wide capacity dispersion within equal-area plans (Section 3.5; Figure 5) underscores that interior plan geometry governs livable capacity as strongly as raw floor area. As illustrated by our partitioned observed showcase (Section 3.1; Figure 2), narrow rooms and poorly placed doorway keep-outs can block both static and transformable layouts even in mid-sized compact flats, whereas well-proportioned smaller shells reach full couple adequacy under S2. Investment in transformable interiors is therefore best targeted at two-person compact dwellings of roughly 12–30 m² NIA—where static layouts bottleneck and identified savings are substantial (Section 3.3)—rather than at single-person observed apartments where static furniture already suffices or at ultra-small micro-shells where operating clearances cannot fit. Furthermore, because widening required circulation from 0.60 m to 0.90 m sharply degrades functional capacity (Section 3.4; Figure S4), architects designing barrier-free or wheelchair-accessible compact dwellings must allocate larger base footprints rather than assuming transformable furniture can absorb wider turning and passage clearances.

For furniture manufacturers and modular fit-out providers, product design should align with both household mechanism and dwelling size band. Couples benefit almost equally from compact product form and daily state switching, so reliable, low-effort day–night wall-bed mechanisms matter most for this household, whereas parent-and-child households gain overwhelmingly from compact multi-state footprints without frequent daily switching (Section 3.4). In drop-one sensitivity tests, the drop-leaf table was the transformable item whose removal cost the most capacity for both two-person schedules (Figure S4). In ultra-compact markets, our Hong Kong parametric kit results demonstrate that furniture dimensions should be selected by floor-area band rather than packaged as universal micro-living kits (Section 3.6): larger couple surfaces expand adequacy in the intermediate band while degrading capacity in smaller shells, whereas downsized single-commuter kits underperform standard catalogue pieces across the grid.

### 4.3. Limitations and Future Work

Three explicit scope boundaries frame the interpretation of our results and guide future research:

1. **Task scope (geometric fit versus experiential quality):** FCI quantifies geometric fit, directional clearances, and continuous walkable reachability for scheduled furniture activities; it does not evaluate acoustic or visual privacy, daylight quality, thermal comfort, ceiling-height perception, or occupant satisfaction. Future post-occupancy surveys and immersive-environment experiments should connect FCI and TB with resident wellbeing and observed daily state-switching adherence to determine how much transformation effort households sustain over time.
2. **Household and catalogue scope:** Our primary schedules and audited furniture catalogue represent stylized daily routines and representative product dimensions rather than exhaustive demographic or commercial diversity. Because plan rasterization, schedules, furniture catalogues, CP-SAT optimization, and independent validation are decoupled in the open pipeline, future studies can directly substitute local time-use diaries, culturally specific domestic routines, regional furniture catalogues, or 0.90 m assistive-mobility circulation rules (Figure S4) without altering the core verification methodology.
3. **Setting scope (Hong Kong model shells versus stock-weighted census totals):** Although Hong Kong's 2021 Population Census documents widespread subdivided-unit occupancy (Section 2.9; Table 1), public census tables omit the joint distribution of household size and unit floor area. Our Hong Kong findings therefore describe the controlled 8–20 m² template grid rather than weighted territory-wide stock totals; linking this evaluation pipeline with measured floor-plan surveys of occupied subdivided units is an immediate priority for policy calibration.

## 5. Conclusions

Across 2619 compact dwellings and four household schedules, this study shows that transformable furniture provides substantial equivalent-area savings where static arrangements bottleneck, but its value is conditional on household composition and dwelling size. Addressing RQ1 (Sections 3.2–3.4), most observed compact plans already satisfy full scheduled demand with static furniture, leaving all-pair median equivalent-area gains at zero on observed plans. Space savings concentrate in two-person households occupying intermediate compact sizes—reaching median identified positive $\Delta\text{EA}$ of 6.21 m² for couples and 5.00 m² for parent-and-child households on observed plans—whereas ultra-small shells lack space even for transformable clearances. Mechanistically, couples gain nearly equally from compact form and daily state switching, while single-person and parent-and-child households gain predominantly from compact form alone.

Addressing RQ2 (Section 3.5), fixed floor-area cutoffs poorly proxy modeled functional adequacy because interior geometry generates wide capacity dispersion within equal-sized plans: a 45 m² two-person cutoff disagrees with S2 adequacy on more than half of observed two-person cases (overwhelmingly false negatives), while an 8 m² floor passes large shares of unworkable micro-shells. Addressing RQ3 (Section 3.6), daily state switching lowers couple minimum adequate area by 2.24 m² in Hong Kong model shells, and parametric kit evaluations show that furniture dimensions should be chosen per floor-area band rather than standardized universally. Together, these findings motivate pairing statutory floor-area minimums with independently verified, schedule-aware layout compliance pathways and targeting transformable design toward two-person compact dwellings of intermediate size.
