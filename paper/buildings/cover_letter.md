Dear Editors of *Buildings*,

We are pleased to submit our original research article, *"Evaluating Transformable Furniture in Compact Housing: Schedule-Aware Equivalent Floor Area and Implications for Minimum Space Standards"*, for consideration in *Buildings* under the section **"Architectural Design, Urban Science, and Real Estate"**.

Across dense cities, shrinking residential footprints are still regulated primarily through fixed internal floor-area minimums, while transformable furniture—such as wall beds, fold-down desks, and drop-leaf tables—is widely promoted to expand livable space. Yet area-only housing rules, static functional space standards, single-room architectural prototypes, and computational layout generators have not established how much usable floor area transformable furniture actually saves across heterogeneous housing stocks and 24-hour household routines.

To answer this question without confounding product capability with manual layout drawing, we evaluate 2619 compact residential plans (2109 observed floor plans from Swiss Dwellings and ResPlan spanning 16.75–50.00 m² net internal area, plus 510 controlled generated shells spanning 8.00–40.00 m²) across four 24-hour household schedules and three nested furniture strategies: static (S0), form only (S1), and form + time (S2). Every dwelling is optimized per strategy via constraint programming and checked by a zero-shared-code independent geometric and 0.60 m walkable-reachability validator, yielding 31,428 verified layouts. We then map paired capacity differences onto the monotone static area–capacity curve to express furniture performance in the square-meter currency of building design and housing policy (equivalent-area gain $\Delta\text{EA}$ and minimum adequate area $A_{\min}$).

Our study establishes four main findings:

1. **Most observed compact plans already function with static furniture.** Static layouts satisfy the entire daily activity schedule in 61.0%–90.8% of observed plans across the four household types, yielding an all-pair median equivalent-area gain of 0 m² on observed plans and showing that transformability acts as a targeted intervention rather than a universal space multiplier.
2. **Space savings concentrate in two-person households of intermediate compact size through distinct physical mechanisms.** Median identified positive equivalent-area gains reach 6.21 m² for couples and 5.00 m² for parent-and-child households on observed plans (peaking in generated shells at 7.32 m² at 14–16 m² for couples and 6.00 m² at 18–20 m² for parent and child), while ultra-small 8–10 m² shells lack space even for transformable clearances. Couples benefit almost equally from compact form and daily state switching, whereas single-person and parent-and-child households gain predominantly from compact form alone.
3. **Floor area alone misjudges functional adequacy.** Because interior room proportions and doorway keep-outs create wide capacity spread at identical areas, a fixed 45 m² two-person benchmark disagrees with modeled S2 adequacy on 55.7% of couple and 52.7% of parent-and-child observed cases (overwhelmingly rejecting workable compact plans), whereas an 8 m² statutory minimum passes unworkable generated shells on 30.0%–49.6% of cases.
4. **Daily state switching and size-band furniture dimensions govern ultra-compact performance in Hong Kong model shells.** Across 390 Hong Kong model shells (8–20 m²), daily state switching reduces minimum adequate area for couples by 2.24 m² (from 18.57 m² to 16.33 m²), and parametric kit testing shows that furniture dimensions should be chosen per floor-area band rather than standardized universally.
